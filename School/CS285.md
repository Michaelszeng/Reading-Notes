## RL
### Fundamentals
- $\mathbf s_t$: State
- $\mathbf o_t$: Observation (by defin. a *subset* of $\mathbf s_t$, if the system is partially observable)
- $\mathbf a_t$: Action
- $\pi_\theta(a_t | o_t)$: observation-conditioned policy
- $\pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)$: state-conditioned, fully observed policy (i.e. $\mathbf o_t = \mathbf s_t$)
- Markov Property: by definition of $\mathbf s$, $\mathbf s_{t+1} \perp \mathbf s_{t-1} ~|~ \mathbf s_t$; i.e. the state contains all information necessary to determine the evolution of the system
- Markov Chain: $M = \{S, T\}$  <--  (state space + transition func.)
	- $T$ is a matrix where $T_{ij} = p(s_{t+1} = i | s_t = j)$$
	- $\mu_{t}$ is a distribution (represented as vector $[\mu_{t,1} \; \mu_{t,2} \; \dots \; \mu_{t,n}]$)
	- $\mu_{t+1} = T \mu_t$
- Markov Decision Process: $M = \{S, T, A, r\}$  <--  (state space, transition func., action space, reward func.)
	- $T$ is now a 3D tensor (current state, next state, current action)
		- $T_{ijk} = p(s_{t+1} = i | s_t = j, a_t = k)$
	- $\mu_{t}$ is a distribution (represented as vector $[\mu_{t,1} \; \mu_{t,2} \; \dots \; \mu_{t,n}]$)
	- $\xi_{t}$ is a distribution over actions (represented as vector $[\xi_{t,1} \; \xi_{t,2} \; \dots \; \xi_{t,m}]$)
	- $\mu_{t+1, i} = \sum_{j, k} T_{ijk} ~ \mu_t ~ \xi_{t,k}$    (probability of next state = $s'$ is sum of all actions times probabilities of that action transitioning into $s'$)
	- $r(s_t, a_t) \rightarrow \mathbb R$
- Observability:
	- In an MDP, we always assume states satisfy Markov property, but observations may not.
	- Partially-observed Markov Decision Process: $M = \{S, T, A, r, O, E\}$  <--  (state space, transition func., action space, reward func., observation space, emission probability)
		- $E = p(o_t | s_t)$ is emission probability

- Goal of RL:
	- Finite-horizon, fully-observed: $$\underbrace{p_\theta(s_1, a_1, \ldots, s_T, a_T)}_{p_\theta(\tau)} = p(s_1)\prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$$
		- Note: we use Markov property to drop dependencies on $s_{t-1}, s_{t-2}, \dots$
		- Define $\tau$ as set of all states/actions
		- Objective: $$\begin{align*} \max_\theta \; \mathbb E_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)} \bigg[\sum_t r(s_t, a_t) \bigg] &= \max_\theta \; \sum_t \mathbb E_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)} [r(s_t, a_t)] \quad \text{(Linearity of Expectation)} \\
						 &= \max_\theta \; \sum_t \mathbb E_{(s_t,a_t) \sim p_\theta(s_t, a_t)}[r(s_t, a_t)] \quad \text{(Since value in summation only depends on } s_t, a_t \text{)}
						\end{align*}$$
	- Infinite-horizon, fully-observed:
		- Assume Markov Chain aperiodic, ergodic; then will reach stationary state distribution $\mu = T \mu$
		- Then, as $T \rightarrow \infty$: $$\max_\theta \; \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \mathbb E_{(s_t,a_t) \sim p_\theta(s_t, a_t)}[r(s_t, a_t)] \quad \rightarrow \quad E_{(s,a) \sim p_\theta(s, a)}[r(s, a)] $$

- Why gradient-based optimization works in RL:
	- Reward func. are often discontinuous
	- But, we maximize expectation under policy's distribution of reward (which is usually smooth/differentiable w.r.t. $\theta$) -- this becomes smooth

### RL Algorithms
**Framework**
1. Generate samples (on-policy or off-policy)
2. Estimate Return (i.e. run value/Q function, or estimate reward from samples)
3. Improve Policy (i.e. gradient descent, or $\arg \max$)

**Types of Algorithms**
- Policy Gradient: take gradient of RL objective w.r.t. $\theta$, run gradient-based optimization to improve $\pi_\theta$
- Value-based: estimate $V$/$Q$ function of the optimal policy (policy is represented implicitly by $\arg\max V$/$Q$)
- Actor-Critic: estimate $V$/$Q$ function of current policy, take gradient w.r.t. $\theta$, run gradient-based optimization to improve $\pi_\theta$
- Model-based RL: estimate transition model of environment, use it for planning (i.e. MPC) or to improve a policy

- Off-policy: able to make use of samples from a different distribution than $\pi_\theta$ to improve the policy (i.e. from teleoperated demonstration, or from an older policy). For sample efficiency

**Value/Q Functions**
- Expand our RL objective: $$ \max_\theta \; \mathbb E_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)} \bigg[\sum_t r(s_t, a_t) \bigg] = \mathbb{E}_{s_1 \sim p(s_1)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{a_1 \sim \pi(a_1 \mid s_1)}\!\left[r(s_1,a_1)+\mathbb{E}_{s_2 \sim p(s_2 \mid s_1,a_1)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{a_2 \sim \pi(a_2 \mid s_2)}\!\left[r(s_2,a_2)+\cdots \mid s_2\right]\mid s_1,a_1 \right] | s_1\right]\right]$$
- We define $Q(s_1, a_1)$: $$Q(s_1, a_1) = r(s_1,a_1)+\mathbb{E}_{s_2 \sim p(s_2 \mid s_1,a_1)}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{a_2 \sim \pi(a_2 \mid s_2)}\!\left[r(s_2,a_2)+\cdots \mid s_2\right]\mid s_1,a_1 \right]$$
	- Expected cumulative reward from being in $s_1$ and taking $a_1$
	- In general: $Q^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) = \sum_{t' = t}^{T} E_{\pi}\left[ r(s_{t'}, a_{t'}) \mid s_t, a_t \right]$
- We define $V(s_1)$: $$\begin{align*}V^\pi(s_t) &= \sum_{t' = t}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta} \left[ r(s_{t'}, a_{t'}) \mid s_t \right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi(a_t \mid s_t)} \left[ Q^\pi(s_t, a_t) \right]\end{align*}$$
	- The RL objective is exactly the $\mathbb{E}_{s_1 \sim p(s_1)}[V(s_1)]$ -- expected value of $s_1$

#### Policy Gradient Algorithm (aka REINFORCE)
- Key Idea: estimate gradient of RL objective w.r.t. $\theta$ and run gradient ascent to improve $\theta$
- Is theoretically guaranteed to converge to a local optimum on the true RL objective (because it directly applies gradient ascent on the RL objective)
**Finite-Horizon**
$$\max_\theta J(\theta), \qquad J(\theta) = \mathbb E_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)} \bigg[\sum_t r(s_t, a_t) \bigg] \quad \text{(Recall } \tau \text{ is the full state/action trajectory over time)}$$
- Key Idea: $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ is intractable to compute directly (we don't know how to sampler from $p_\theta(\tau)$ since the environment/transition func. is unknown)
	- However, we can use algebra to show we only need to compute $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)$
	- $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)$ is computationally tractable bc we have an exact expression of $\pi_\theta$ (can use typical auto-diff/backprop)
		<center><img src="School Supplements/cs285_policy_gradient_derivation.png" alt="" style="width:800px; margin-top: 10px"/></center>
	- While the expectation is still intractable, we can estimate it well using MC sampling.
			<img src="School Supplements/cs285_reinforce_alg.png" alt="" style="width:450px; margin-top: 10px; padding-left: 25px"/>
- Interpretation as a "reweighted" MLE:
			<center><img src="School Supplements/cs285_policy_gradient_as_reweighted_mle.png" alt="" style="width:600px; margin-top: 10px"/></center>
	- Mathematically, policy gradient looks like a re-weighted maximum likelihood gradient.
	- In supervised learning, MLE maximizes log probabilities of all $N$ samples from dataset. We assume all samples from dataset are equally good.
	- In policy gradient, we maximize log probabilities of $N$ sampled rollouts, weighted by the reward gained by that sample. 
- KEY PROBLEM: MC estimates are high variance
	- Policy-gradient is exact with infinite samples, but in finite-sample regime, MC estimate of the gradient is high variance, very noisy. In practice, pathological changes (i.e. adding constants to the reward function) affect behavior significantly.
	- Variance-Reduction Techniques:
		-  Introduce causality: realize that, for any sample $\tau$, the gradient induced by $\pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)$ should only be weighed by the rewards at $t'$ later than $t$ (i.e. $t' \ge t$)
			$$ \begin{align*} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &\approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t}) \right) \left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} r(s_{i,t}, a_{i,t}) \right) \\ 
				&= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}  \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t}) \left( \sum_{t'=1}^{T} r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'}) \right) \quad \text{(Distribute } \left( \sum_{t'=1}^T r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'}) \right) \text{ across each } \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t}) \text{ term)}\\
				&\approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}  \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t}) \underbrace{\left( \sum_{t'=t}^{T} r(s_{i,t'}, a_{i,t'}) \right)}_{\text{``reward to go"}} \quad \text{(Modify second sum to start at } t \text{)} \end{align*}$$
			- This modifies the estimator, though it is still unbiased.
			- Note that, w/ infinite-samples, the impact of earlier rewards on $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ should cancel out to 0. But this helps in finite-sample case.
		- Subtract a constant baseline reward: Instead of using $r(\tau)$, use $(r(\tau) - b)$. This doesn't bias our estimate of $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$: $$\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, r(\tau) \right] \underbrace{=}_{\text{we prove this}} \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, (r(\tau) - b) \right]$$
			- Proof: $$\begin{align*} \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, (r(\tau) - b) \right] &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, r(\tau) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, b \right] \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, b \right] \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - \int p_\theta(\tau) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\, b\ d\tau \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - \int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\, b\ d\tau \quad \text{(Apply derivative rule for} \ln(x) \text{)} \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - b \nabla_\theta \int p_\theta(\tau)\ d\tau \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - b \nabla_\theta \int p_\theta(\tau)\ d\tau \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - b \nabla_\theta 1 \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta)\end{align*}$$
			- What do we choose for $b$?
				- Common choice in practice: $b = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r(\tau)$   (average reward)
				- We can derive the *optimal* $b$ (for lowest variance):
					- Recall: $\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log p_{\theta}(\tau)\, (r(\tau) - b) \right]$ 					$$\begin{align*} \mathrm{Var}(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)) &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\left(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)(r(\tau)-b)\right)^2\right] - \left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)(r(\tau)-b)\right]\right)^2 \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\left(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)(r(\tau)-b)\right)^2\right] - \left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)r(\tau)\right]\right)^2 \end{align*}$$
					- Optimize w.r.t. $b$:  $$\begin{align*} \frac{d \mathrm{Var}}{db} &= \frac{d}{db} \bigg( \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\left(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)(r(\tau)-b)\right)^2\right] - \left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)r(\tau)\right]\right)^2 \bigg) \\ 
						&= \frac{d}{db} \bigg( \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\left(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)(r(\tau)-b)\right)^2\right] \bigg) \quad \text{(Second term is constant w.r.t. } b \text{)} \\ 
						&= \frac{d}{db} \bigg( \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2 r(\tau)^2\right]}_{\text{becomes 0; is constant w.r.t. } b} - 2b ~ \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2 r(\tau)\right] + b^2 ~ \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2\right] \bigg) \quad \text{(Distribute the square)} \\
						&= -2\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2 r(\tau)\right] + 2b ~ \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2\right] = 0 \\
						& \implies b = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2 r(\tau)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau))^2 \right]}\end{align*} $$
					- Intuition: optimal $b$ is the expected/average reward, weighted by samples with high gradients (i.e. $b$ is more weighted for samples inducing large gradients, thus canceling those heavy gradients more/balancing the gradients from all the samples) (?)
					- Note used in practice b.c. $\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)$ hard to estimate itself
- Off-policy policy gradient algorithm
	- Key: importance reweighting using samples from off-policy distribution $\bar p(\tau)$ instead of $p_\theta(\tau)$ $$J(\theta) = \mathbb E_{\tau \sim \bar p(\tau)} \bigg[ \frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{\bar p(\tau)} r(\tau) \bigg]$$

## Behavior Cloning: Methods
Representing multi-modal distributions
1. Diffusion models (*Diffusion Policy*)
2. CVAEs (i.e. "latent-space models") (*Action Chunking Transformer*)
3. Discretization (specifically, auto-regressive discretization to make it scalable) (*Google RT-1*)
	- Main idea: # bins increases exponentially w/dimension. Instead of predicting all dimensions at once, predict dimensions autoregressively
	- i.e. in 3-bin, 3-dim case: <center><img src="School Supplements/cs285_autoregressive_discretization.png" alt="" style="width:350px; margin-top: 10px"/></center>
		- Why this works: autoregressive prediction is equivalent to one-step prediction:
			- $p(a_{t,0} | s_t) ~ p(a_{t,1} | s_t, a_{t,0}) ~ p(a_{t,2} | s_t, a_{t,0}, a_{t,1}) = p(a_{t_0}, a_{t,1}, a_{t,2} | s_t)$   <--   Chain Rule of Probability
	- Cons: require heavy-weight sequence models + requires discretization

## Principles of Behavior Cloning
- Classic supervised learning objective: (MLE on predicting the data distribution):
$$ \max_\theta \mathbb E_{o_t \sim p_{data}(o_t)}[\log \pi_\theta(a_t | o_t)]$$
- What we actually care about in behavior cloning (minimizing the number of "mistakes" the policy makes in the distribution of states it induces during execution ($c(s_t, a_t)$ returns penalty of 1 per mistake):
$$\min_\theta \mathbb E_{s_t \sim p_{\pi_\theta(s_t)}} [c(s_t, a_t)], \qquad c(s_t, a_t) = \cases{0 \quad\text{ if } a_t = \pi^*(s_t) \\ 1 \quad\text{ otherwise}}$$
- NOTATION NOTE: Assume deterministic policy -- so $\pi^*(s)$ returns an action.
- Fundamental difference between supervised learning and behavior cloning: distribution shift.
	- Supervised learning relies on i.i.d. data samples assumption; but in behavior cloning, previous robot actions heavily influence future states/observations.
	- Worst case: "tight-rope walker"
		- Consider an episode length $T$; at every time step, only 1 correct action $\pi^*(s_t)$. Taking an incorrect action leads to unrecoverable task failure.
		- Assume $p(\pi_\theta(s_t) \ne \pi^*(s_t)) \le \epsilon \quad \forall t$
			$$ \mathbb E \bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T c(s_t, a_t) \bigg] \le \epsilon T + (1-\epsilon T)\big( \epsilon(T-1) + (1-\epsilon)(\epsilon(T-2) + \dots)\big) = O(\epsilon T^2)$$
		- The # mistakes scales quadratically with $T$. 
	- We can show that $O(\epsilon T^2)$ is an upper bound on $\mathbb E \bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T c(s_t, a_t) \bigg]$ for any problem:
		- Assuming: $\Pr((a_t \sim \pi_\theta) \ne \pi^*(s_t) | s_t) \le \epsilon \quad \forall s \sim p_{train}(s)$
			- Expected probability of predicting any action not equal to the optimal action $\le \epsilon$
		- First, we prove a bound: $\| p_\theta(s_t) - p_{\text{train}}(s_t) \|_{TV} \le 2 \left( 1 - (1 - \epsilon)^t \right) \le 2 \epsilon t$
			- $p_\theta(s_t) = (1 - \epsilon)^t \, p_{\text{train}}(s_t) \;+\; \bigl(1 - (1 - \epsilon)^t\bigr)\, p_{\text{mistake}}(s_t)$
				- First term is probability of not making any mistakes in $t$ steps (thus $s_t$ is in training distribution)
				- Second term is probability of having made at least 1 mistake in $t$ steps (thus $s_t$ in some other "mistake" distribution)
			- Re-arrange: $\| p_\theta(s_t) - p_{\text{train}}(s_t) \|_{TV} = \left( 1 - (1 - \epsilon)^t \right) \| p_{\text{mistake}}(s_t) - p_{\text{train}}(s_t) \|_{TV}$
				- Recall TV distance: $\|\mu - \nu \|_{TV} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} |\mu(x) - \nu(x)|$ ; recall TV distance bounded $\le 2$ for any 2 distributions
				- $\| p_\theta(s_t) - p_{\text{train}}(s_t) \|_{TV} \le 2 \left( 1 - (1 - \epsilon)^t \right) \le 2 \epsilon t$
					- (Last inequality comes from identity: $(1-\epsilon)^t \ge 1 - \epsilon t$)
		- Final bound: $$ \begin{align*} \mathbb E_{s_t \sim p_\theta, a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | s_t)} \bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T c(s_t, a_t) \bigg] &= \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{s_t \sim p_\theta} \bigg[ \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim\pi_\theta} [c(s_t, a_t)] \bigg] \quad \text{(Move sum out of expectation; apply Law of Total Expectation)} \\
				&= \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{s_t \sim p_\theta} [ c(s_t) ] \quad \text{(Define } c(s_t) := \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim\pi_\theta} [c(s_t)] = \Pr((a_t \sim \pi_\theta) \ne \pi^*(s_t) | s_t) \text{)}\\
				&= \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{s_t} p_\theta(s_t) ~c(s_t) \\ 
				&= \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{s_t} (p_\theta(s_t) + p_{train}(s_t) - p_{train}(s_t)) ~c(s_t)  \\ 
				&\le \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{s_t} \big( p_{train}(s_t) ~c(s_t) + \|p_\theta(s_t) - p_{train}(s_t)\|_{TV} ~c(s_t) \big) \quad \text{(Distribute } c(s_t) \text{; absolute difference always greater than difference)}\\
				&\le \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{s_t} p_{train}(s_t) ~c(s_t) + \sum_{T=1}^T2 \epsilon t \quad \text{(Plug in bound } \| p_\theta(s_t) - p_{\text{train}}(s_t) \|_{TV} \le 2 \epsilon t \text{ from above, use the fact that } c(s_t)\le1 \text{)} \\
				&\le \sum_{T=1}^T \epsilon + 2 \epsilon t \quad \text{(By defn., combined probability mass of being in ``bad" states that incur cost } c(s_t)=1 \text{ is } \epsilon \text{)} \\
				&\le  \epsilon T + 2 \epsilon T^2 = O(\epsilon T^2)\end{align*}$$
	- KEY IDEA: with DAgger, $\mathbb E \bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T c(s_t, a_t) \bigg] \le O(\epsilon T)$
		- Proof:
			- If you do enough DAgger: $p_{train} = p_\theta$, i.e. state distribution during train and test are the same
			- From above: $$ \begin{align*} \mathbb E_{s_t \sim p_\theta, a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | s_t)} \bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T c(s_t, a_t) \bigg] &= \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{s_t} \big( p_{train}(s_t) ~c(s_t) + \|p_\theta(s_t) - p_{train}(s_t)\|_{TV} ~c(s_t) \big) \\
					&= \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{s_t} p_{train}(s_t) ~c(s_t) \quad \text{(Since } p_\theta = p_{train} \text{)} \\
					&\le \sum_{t=1}^T \epsilon \quad \text{(By defn., combined probability mass of being in ``bad" states that incur cost } c(s_t)=1 \text{ is } \epsilon \text{)} \\
					&= O(\epsilon T)
					\end{align*}$$
